Jacob Krol Area Exam

Structural variants (SVs)

  • Structural variants are primary variation I will discuss
  • They are large mutations deviating from expected human genome sequence
  • SVs types characterize how seq. varies from expected
  • DEL: loss of sequence
  • DUP: gain of existing seq.
  • INV: reversal of order
  • INS: gain of novel seq.
  • TRANS: sequence misplaced compared to expected

Large base pair effect size of SVs

  • SVs are important because they contribute a large amount of total bp variation per genome
  • Estimates vary, but genome.gov estimates >20Mbp of variation on avg.
  • However, # SV events small (~25k) compared to SNPs and INDELs

  • SVs often causes disease
  • Especially, neurological, developmental, and cancer diseases
  • Therefore, there’s clinical incentive to understand SVs:
    • What mechanisms cause them?
    • When do SV mechanisms occur?
    • Do they occur in multiple individuals (recurrency)?
    • Are they pathological?

Computation necessity

  • Computational approaches are necessary to answer these questions
  • For example, a single tumor sample’s binary format sequence alignment map can be over 100GB
  • Disease analysis requires large samples sizes of both control and condition
  • Therefore, sheer data size necessitates computational analysis

SV mechanisms

SV recipe

  1. Where: homology, repeats, double-strand breaks, and replication forks
  2. When: synthesis, recombination, or repair
  • SV mechanisms vary by where and when they occur
  • Where: repeats, damaged DNA, or replication forks
  • When: synthesis, recombination, or repair

Allelic homologous recombination

The first mechanism involves recombination process where DNA is exchanged between homologous chromosomes at the same location or allele.

Non-allelic homologous recombination

  • However, ‘non-allelic’ homologous recombination is crossing over at two different sites causing SV
  • For ex, 2 homologous chromosomes might line up incorrectly due to repeat homology
  • In the left example, the AB alleles of the 1st chromosome abnormally lines up with the CD alleles of the 2nd chromosome
  • This may cause a DEL in one chromosome and a DUP in the other

Smith-Magenis syndrome


NAHR SV near RAI1 and nearby genes on CHR 17


  • Smith-Magenis syndrome is hypothesized to be caused by NAHR nearby the RAI1 gene
  • Symptoms include physical and developmental abnormalities

Segmental duplications drive NAHR


  • Segmental duplications (SDs) recruit NAHR
  • ~5% of human genome
  • High identity (>~95%)
  • Large (>~10kbp)

  • A canonical homologous element is the segmental duplication
  • These are large highly homologous (high seq. similarity) elements
  • The area around these elements is known to be a hotspot for NAHR

NAHR DEL-DUP

  • How exactly does this mechanism work?
  • Say we have 2 chromosomes with 2 large, homologous repeats on each

NAHR DEL-DUP

  • The recombination window might abnormally form like this
  • And after crossing over, the top chromosome will only receive 1 repeat copy, while the bottom chr receives an extra third repeat copy

NAHR elements and risk


Homologous element Min. Seq. identitiy Min. length

  • Risk \(\propto\) length, sequence identity, closeness
  • Beyond SDs, other genomic elements are known to cause SVs via NAHR: LINE and HERV
  • The SV risk of NAHR increases as the repeats are larger or more similar
  • The SV risk also increases the closer these elments are

Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)

  • Another mechanism is non-homolgous-end joining
  • When DNA broken, then imperfectly repaired

Chronic myeloid leukemia fusion

  • NHEJ SV hypothesis (minimal breakpoint homology)
  • Recurrency hypothesis: CHR9 and CHR22 regions are physically close
  • For example, in chronic myeloid leukemia, the tumor driving fusion BCR-ABL is hyp. to be from NHEJ
  • Although NHEJ is not typically recurrent, the hypothesis is that CHR9 and CHR22 gene regions are physically close, and suscetible to abnormal repair deriving a fusion event

NHEJ example

  • A common complex SV formed by NHEJ is a DUP, TRP, DUP event when a replication fork collapses unexpectedly during DNA synthesis
    1. one replication fork collapses
    1. the loose end invades another replication fork
    1. synthesis begins again
    1. The second replication collapses and a dsDNA break occurs
  • finally, synthesized strand invades the break producing a large copy number gain

NHEJ summary


  • Imperfect repair of double strand DNA break
  • Minimal homology at breakpoints
  • Example: BCR-ABL1 fusion CML
  • Usually low recurrency

Break-induced replication (BIR)



  • Replication fork collapses
  • Abnormal loose end of synth. strand
  • Template switch over to previous repeat copy
  • Result is a duplication

Fork stalling and template switching (FoSTeS)

Template switching during replication


  • Replication
  • Stalling (e.g., secondary structure)
  • Lagging strand frees
  • Invades another replication fork

MAPS intution

Is SNV functional class enriched with rare (singleton) events?

MAPS

  • MAPS: Mutability adjusted proportion of singletons
  • Aggregate singletons within SNV functional classes (e.g., pLoF or missense)
  • MAPS \(\approx\) Observed singleton prop. \(-\text{ }f\)(expected singleton prop.)
  • MAPS > 0: more singletons observed than expected
  • MAPS: Mutability adjusted proportion of singletons
  • Aggregate singletons within SNV functional classes (e.g., pLoF or missense)
  • MAPS > 0: more singletons observed than expected

LOEUF

  • LOEUF: Loss-of-function observed expected upper bound fraction
  • Goal: mesaure intolerance to pLoF
  • Approach: score genes by pLoF SNV proportion
  • Metric: #Obs. pLoF SNVs / #Exp. pLoF SNVs; take upper bound CI
  • LOEUF metric is “Loss-of-function observed expected upper bound fraction”
  • Goal: mesaure intolerance to pLoF
  • approach: score genes by pLoF SNV proportion
  • value: #Obs. pLoF SNVs / #Exp. pLoF SNVs; take upper bound CI
  • A lower value implies a gene constrained to not have LoF variants
  • The confidence interval allows better resolution for small and large genes, such as the example in text

Fusion calling strategy

  • 18 alignment-based methods
  • 5 assembly-based methods
  • There are two strategy types to calling fusions
    1. raw read alignemnt (short contigs)
    1. Transcript assembly then alignment
  • the units of fusion evidence are i) discordant/spanning reads and ii) split/junction reads
  • Discordant reads are specific to paired-end technology: each mate maps fully to another gene
  • Split reads are evidence in single or paired-end read techs: the read partially maps to both genes

Fusion calling evaluation

  • Evaluation is done via classification metrics to both compare tools and select parameters (e.g., min. supporting reads per call)
  • Problem: full truth set is typically unknown
  • NEXT
  • Precision = TP/
  • Recall = TP / P
  • F1 combines recall and precision

Simulated evaluation

  • However, in simulation the truth set is known
  • The performance summary here is that
  • read methods outperform the assembly methods (Trinity and Jaffa-assembly)
  • most methods performed better with longer read lengths, except SOAPFuse and Fusionhunter (older tools probably tuned to older seq. tech with smaller read lens)
  • overall best performers were Arriba, pizzly, STAR-Fusion (highest auPRC)
  • and panel b shows higher fusion expression led to higher recall

Cell line evaluation

  • For real data, they started with 4 cell lines that had experimentally validated fusions

  • recall was highest for tools that also performed best in simulated eval

  • left VA is recall of validated fusions, bars
